# IDNE Design Philosophy

**Purpose:** Define the lasting identity of the Interactive Detective Narrative Engine.  
**Scope:** Philosophy only — not rules, schemas, validators, or adventure content.  
**Source:** Real two-player playtest discoveries, separated from implementation examples.  
**Audience:** Future engine authors, adventure designers, and compilers — including after a full rewrite.

---

## 1. Identity in one sentence

**IDNE simulates a living mystery world so players can investigate like detectives with a Dungeon Master — not read a branching novel with page numbers.**

---

## 2. Category A — Permanent design philosophy

These principles should remain true even if the engine is rewritten from scratch.

---

### A1. Fair mystery: reconstruct, do not receive

**Statement:** The case must be logically solvable from evidence that was fairly obtainable. Guilt must not be obvious from a single gift. Motive and method must be reconstructed by connecting discoveries.

**Why better detective gameplay:** Detection is the act of assembling meaning. Handed conclusions collapse the genre into a tour.

**Why better immersion:** Players feel smart when *they* form the theory; immersion dies when the book forms it for them.

**Why not a traditional gamebook:** Gamebooks often deliver “you find the letter that solves everything.” IDNE prefers incomplete fragments that only resolve when combined.

**Why it supports the DM vision:** A DM never dumps the solution; they place evidence in the world and wait for questions.

**Playtest root:** The mystery itself worked; the failure was clues given instead of discovered and connected.

---

### A2. Equal narrative weight for all suspects

**Statement:** No suspect’s introduction, diction, or stage business may unintentionally signal narrative importance. Every major suspect must feel equally believable as the culprit until evidence narrows the field.

**Why better detective gameplay:** Premature certainty ends investigation. Equal believability forces evidence-based elimination.

**Why better immersion:** Real cases do not announce the murderer with tone. Players trust the world when tone stays neutral.

**Why not a traditional gamebook:** Many gamebooks telegraph the villain with adjectives, nervous ticks reserved for one person, or spotlight placement.

**Why it supports the DM vision:** A good DM does not wink at the camera. Suspicion arises from facts, not from authorial emphasis.

**Playtest root:** A player immediately suspected the culprit from subtle introductory wording — a writing failure.

---

### A3. Suspicious innocents are a feature

**Statement:** Innocent people may behave nervously, evasively, or inconveniently. Guilt is not required for suspicious behaviour. Realistic red herrings come from human pressure, fear, and self-interest — not from fake planted nonsense.

**Why better detective gameplay:** Players must distinguish “suspicious” from “guilty.” That skill *is* detective work.

**Why better immersion:** Real people under institutional pressure look guilty. The world feels adult and honest.

**Why not a traditional gamebook:** Classic red herrings are often cartoonish decoys. IDNE prefers people who would act that way even if no murder occurred.

**Why it supports the DM vision:** NPCs have motives independent of the plot beat. The DM plays people, not props.

**Playtest root:** Nervous innocents felt good and created realistic red herrings.

---

### A4. Believable behaviour over dramatic convenience

**Statement:** Realism outweighs drama. Characters must not act unnaturally solely because the plot needs a twist, a confession, or a cliffhanger. Surprising outcomes are welcome only when they remain psychologically and institutionally credible.

**Why better detective gameplay:** If NPCs break character for plot, evidence becomes unreliable and “fair play” collapses.

**Why better immersion:** Players stay inside the fiction when people behave like people.

**Why not a traditional gamebook:** Gamebooks often sacrifice psychology for set-piece drama. IDNE refuses that trade.

**Why it supports the DM vision:** The world continues by its own rules. The DM does not puppet NPCs into theatrical beats.

**Playtest root:** Explicit preference: realism > drama; believable behaviour > surprising behaviour.

---

### A5. Player-directed investigation

**Statement:** Players decide what to investigate. The engine presents a world of available actions and information, not a tour itinerary. “What do *you* want to look at?” is the core question. “Go here next” is a failure mode.

**Why better detective gameplay:** Agency is the difference between solving and being led. Direction kills ownership of the theory.

**Why better immersion:** Detectives choose their next move. Being escorted destroys the fantasy of competence.

**Why not a traditional gamebook:** Gamebooks are rail-and-branch: choose page A or B. IDNE aims for open inquiry within constraints.

**Why it supports the DM vision:** Players ask; the DM answers. The book does not ask the players which chapter to open by code.

**Playtest root:** Structure was good, but the game told players where to go.

---

### A6. Dungeon Master simulator, not branching novel

**Statement:** IDNE’s long-term identity is a Dungeon Master for detective fiction: simulate the world, resolve actions, advance time, update knowledge. It is not primarily a branching novel, choose-your-own-adventure, or page-turning gamebook — even if early prototypes use print-friendly delivery.

**Why better detective gameplay:** Simulation lets many actions exist; branching novels force a few authored forks. Detectives need breadth.

**Why better immersion:** Players inhabit a place that reacts, not a script that pre-writes their path.

**Why not a traditional gamebook:** Gamebooks encode story as graph edges. A DM encodes story as world state + responses to intent.

**Why it supports the DM vision:** This *is* the vision. Delivery format may be books, apps, or hybrid — identity is simulation.

**Playtest root:** Explicit long-term goal stated by owners after play.

---

### A7. Scarcity creates decisions (especially time)

**Statement:** Meaningful choice comes primarily from resource limits — especially limited time — not from artificial binary forks. Prefer many possible actions and insufficient time to do them all. Scarcity forces prioritization; forced branching forces menu-picking.

**Why better detective gameplay:** “We can interview three people or crack the logs — not both before the deadline” is a real investigative tradeoff.

**Why better immersion:** Real investigations are races against sealing, memory, and politics. Menus are not.

**Why not a traditional gamebook:** Gamebooks manufacture drama with A/B doors. IDNE manufactures drama with opportunity cost.

**Why it supports the DM vision:** A DM rarely says “pick ending branch 2.” A DM says “the building locks in an hour — what do you do?”

**Playtest root:** Time identified as one of the strongest mechanics; resource limits preferred over forced branching.

---

### A8. Discovery and connection over delivery

**Statement:** Clues should be found through action and connected through reasoning. The engine may reveal observations; it should not routinely reveal conclusions. The player’s job is synthesis.

**Why better detective gameplay:** Connection is the skill. Delivery is sightseeing.

**Why better immersion:** The “aha” belongs to the player, not the narrator.

**Why not a traditional gamebook:** Many gamebooks print the theory for you when you reach the right page.

**Why it supports the DM vision:** DMs describe what you see; they do not announce what it means unless asked and justified.

**Playtest root:** Mystery worked; clues were handed instead of discovered and connected.

---

### A9. Cooperative mystery, not parallel solo novels

**Statement:** When two players cooperate, the intended experience is shared investigation with meaningful separation and reunions — not two separate adventures that occasionally swap notes. Separation creates perspective; it must not erase partnership.

**Why better detective gameplay:** Shared scenes create joint theories; pure split creates two incomplete monologues.

**Why better immersion:** Partners solve cases together in fiction and life. Solo tracks feel like homework.

**Why not a traditional gamebook:** Dual gamebooks often maximize private pages. IDNE maximizes *case* ownership together.

**Why it supports the DM vision:** A DM runs one table. Players split and rejoin; the table remains one investigation.

**Playtest root:** Adventure felt almost entirely split; intended experience is shared + meaningful splits + frequent regrouping.

*(Elevated to philosophy because the intended cooperative identity was stated as desired experience, not as a one-off balance tweak.)*

---

### A10. Neutrality of presentation

**Statement:** The engine and narrator must not steer players toward “recommended” actions, “correct” suspects, or preferred routes. Influence belongs to evidence and pressure inside the fiction — never to meta advice.

**Why better detective gameplay:** Steering collapses fair play into guided tourism.

**Why better immersion:** Meta recommendations shatter the fourth wall of investigation.

**Why not a traditional gamebook:** Gamebooks often label “best” paths or hint heavily. IDNE refuses authorial coaching.

**Why it supports the DM vision:** A DM may warn of danger in-world; they do not say “you should pick option A.”

**Playtest root:** Recommended choices felt forbidden and broke agency.

## 3. Category B — Implementation decisions

These are valid *examples* or tactics. They may change between adventures, formats, or eras of the engine. They must not be mistaken for identity.

| Item | Why it is implementation, not philosophy |
|---|---|
| Print booklets with scene codes (`J-100`, `S-210`) | A delivery format for an early prototype — not the product identity |
| Fixed split windows with wait-until-sync | One sync model among many possible DM-style time models |
| D20 skill checks and DC bands | A resolution toy; other resolution systems can serve the same philosophy |
| Named trust scalars (`T_*`) | One way to track rapport; could be stage tags, marks, or purely diegetic |
| Sixteen numbered clue cards | A packaging choice; clues could be freeform notes or digital tags |
| Five terminal ending families | An adventure structure choice; count and shape may vary |
| Corporate campus / modern industrial setting | Adventure content, not engine identity |
| Exactly two players | Current primary use case; philosophy of investigation survives solo or larger tables if the DM model holds |
| Explicit regroup scenes as separate “chapters” | One pacing device; freeform “meet when you choose” is also compatible with A5–A7 |
| Public Static Node / gamebook compiler pipeline | An architecture for producing printables — subordinate to the DM simulation vision |
| Participation audits and structural validators | Process tools; they serve philosophy only if they measure experience, not file counts |
| In-world clock labels (19:00–00:30) | Adventure-specific timeline shape |
| “Medium difficulty / ~2 hours” targets | Product sizing, not identity |

**Rule of thumb:** If changing it would not threaten “DM-simulated fair mystery,” it belongs in Category B.

## 4. Contradictions with the *current* engine (as lived)

These are identity conflicts observed through play and through how the engine has been used — stated as philosophy vs practice, not as a compliance ticket list.

| Philosophy | Current practice tends toward | Tension |
|---|---|---|
| A6 DM simulator | Graph-first gamebook compilation, page navigation | Product identity diluted into branching novel |
| A5 Player-directed investigation | “Go to S-210” menus, recommended splits | Players follow itineraries |
| A7 Scarcity decisions | Artificial binary forks with effects spoiled on the choice page | Menus replace opportunity cost |
| A8 Discovery and connection | Auto-granted clue dumps on scene entry | Touring, not detecting |
| A9 Cooperative shared case | Split-majority scene graphs | Two solo books |
| A10 Neutrality | “Recommended” choices, steering language | Author coaching |
| A2 Equal suspect weight | Uneven introductory emphasis (playtest: early suspect from wording) | Tone leaks |
| A7 / A1 Time as pressure | Clock text without felt option change | Time as flavour, not mechanic |

**Philosophical stance:** Where current architecture and the DM vision conflict, **the DM vision wins**. Delivery layers may remain print-friendly, but they are servants, not masters.

## 5. Missing philosophical principles

Playtest and owner intent imply these permanent principles even when not numbered in the discovery list:

### A11. Fair play is sacred

Players must be able to reach a justified solution from information that was obtainable in play. Retcon, narrator lies about sensory facts, and impossible obligations are out of identity.

### A12. Knowledge ≠ truth

The world has a fixed truth; players and NPCs hold incomplete or wrong beliefs. Investigation is the closing of that gap — never the rewriting of history for drama.

### A13. The world continues without the players

NPCs, institutions, and clocks advance for their own reasons. Waiting is not free; absence has consequences. This is the DM’s off-screen clock.

### A14. Failure changes the path; it rarely erases the case

Missed chances and failed attempts should raise cost, reduce certainty, or close *a* door — not silently delete the only fair solution unless a fair failure ending was an intentional, visible risk.

### A15. Private information exists to create perspective, not busywork

Separation is for asymmetric discovery and reunion synthesis. It is not for padding page count or forcing idle waiting as “content.”

### A16. The engine does not congratulate or coach

No recommended routes, no “you should accuse X,” no meta wink. Praise and blame belong inside outcomes earned by play.

### A17. Emotional investment comes from stakes in the world, not from twist density

Suspense arises from deadline pressure, human cost, institutional threat, and uncertain alliances — not from mandatory mid-act fireworks.

---

## 6. What the playtest did **not** reveal

Important unknowns that philosophy should not pretend to settle:

| Open question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Solo DM-simulation model | Two-player was tested; solo identity is still underspecified |
| How open is “open investigation”? | Full sandbox vs constrained action menus vs free text intent — unresolved |
| Digital vs print primacy | Philosophy allows both; no playtest of a live simulator |
| How much combat / coercion / illegality | Realism vs playable ethics boundaries untouched |
| Facilitator / AI DM in the loop | Human DM, AI DM, or pure static artifact — not tested |
| Long campaigns / multi-case | Only one ~hour case was played |
| Accessibility and language localization | Difficulty complained about; no systematic language philosophy stress test |
| Competitive or asymmetric goals | Pure coop tested; betrayal/competition unknown |
| How endings should *feel* when correct | Disconnect observed; ideal “earned certainty” ritual not yet defined by play |
| Minimum shared % as a number | Philosophy says shared matters; exact ratio is Category B |

Do not freeze Category B numbers into Category A until more playtests exist.

## 8. Short creed (printable)

1. Simulate a world; do not author a tour.  
2. Players investigate; the book does not investigate for them.  
3. Evidence is found and connected; conclusions are earned.  
4. Every suspect can be believed; tone never winks.  
5. Innocent people may look guilty; that is realism.  
6. Behaviour stays believable even when the plot wants drama.  
7. Time and scarcity create decisions; menus are last resorts.  
8. Partners share a case; splits create perspective, not isolation.  
9. Never coach the “right” move.  
10. If it only works as a branching novel, it is not yet IDNE.

---

## 9. Closing

IDNE’s identity is not “a better gamebook.”

It is **a fair, realistic detective DM**: a world that keeps moving, people who behave like people, evidence that must be earned and connected, and players who choose what matters before the clock runs out.

Everything else — formats, dice, page codes, compilers, validators — is implementation.

*End of philosophy document.*
